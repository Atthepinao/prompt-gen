# Ship prompt samples — post-rewrite showcase

These are sample outputs from the rewritten `core/ship_generator.py`,
covering the new 12-archetype taxonomy and 10-variant set, with and
without designer attributions.

Each prompt uses narrative prose, no NEGATIVE/FORBIDDEN sections,
places SUBJECT + DESIGNER NAME in the high-weight first 50 words,
and inlines the carrier launch directive as positive description
rather than as an anti-flat-deck negative block.

## Adventurer Tramp / Civilian / Kawamori

_880 words_

Hand-painted Japanese mecha OVA cel artwork in the visual tradition of late-1980s to mid-1990s capital-ship illustration. The subject is a basic-grade small private long-range adventurer / tramp / privateer ship — civilian-registered hull operating at the edge of the law, the kind of small crewed ship a freelance crew would call home. Scale: small civilian craft — roughly 30 to 60 meters long, comparable in scale to a private yacht or small cargo runner. The vessel is designed by Shoji Kawamori.

The designer's distinctive visual vocabulary drives every silhouette decision: Aerospace-derived bipedal frame (head-to-body ~1:7): forward-pointing aerodynamic prow integrated into the chest, swing-wing aero panels stowed against the back as a backpack, leg modules clearly shaped as engine nacelles with afterburner rings at the heels. Surface treatment: aerospace skin — tight panel lines following stress paths, hinged control surfaces, reaction-control thrusters dotted around the body, air-data probes, formation strip lights, low-vis tactical greys with small squadron decals. Sensor module styled like a fighter aircraft cockpit canopy. Intake ducts on the chest or hips. Foldable wings carrying conformal external stores at the roots. Reverse-knee posing capability for transitional travel modes. NEVER baroque trim, NEVER ceremonial swords as primary armament, NEVER medieval silhouettes, NEVER anything that cannot plausibly transform into a flying configuration. The defining visual anchor that must dominate the silhouette is: compact roughly 30–60 meter civilian hull with a clearly visible single bubble or stepped cockpit / bridge canopy at the dorsal forward position, an obvious main hatch / cargo ramp on the underside or stern, and an oversized rear engine block that is dramatically larger than the rest of the vessel — silhouette reads as a personal lived-in starship, NOT a warship and NOT a fighter. Overall silhouette: stubby civilian hull, dorsal forward bridge bubble, oversized rear engine block hanging off the stern, side-mounted folding landing gear bays, asymmetric bolt-on equipment panels giving the impression of a vessel maintained by its crew rather than a navy. The hull reads as three clearly differentiated longitudinal acts along its long axis. The forward third is an aggressive prow carrying the heaviest concentration of weapons, sensors, or launch infrastructure, setting the vessel's direction. The middle third is a vertically stacked multi-tier armored superstructure, visually denser and taller than the bow or stern, bristling with antenna masts, sensor dishes, and secondary turrets — this is the visual focal point. The aft third is a thick armored engine block whose multiple large thruster bells are partially recessed into the armor plate, with internal framework only glimpsed through armor cutouts and access panels rather than hung off external scaffolding.

Surface detail is dense and reads as a true capital-ship illustration. Hundreds of small lighted viewports, portholes, and access hatches are scattered across the entire hull surface, sized as human-scale crew ports — they are the implicit scale reference that makes the vessel read as 500 to 2000 meters long. Every large armor face is subdivided by panel-line work into smaller sub-panels with clear seam lines and rivet courses. Antenna mast forests, parabolic dish clusters, and sensor arrays bristle from the dorsal spine. Conduit channels, panel seams, and inset cable trays live within the armor envelope. Stenciled hull numbers, painted unit markings, and warning chevron stripes punctuate the surface as accent-color details. The primary hull volume is strictly left/right symmetric, while small bolted-on modules, antenna mounts, patch panels, and equipment pods are placed asymmetrically to give the vessel a lived-in production history. The hull is at least four times longer than it is tall. Visible structural features include oversized rear engine block with two or three large thruster bells, dramatically scaled up relative to the hull, dorsal forward bridge canopy or stepped command bubble, asymmetric bolt-on equipment pods, fuel tanks or salvage rigs welded onto the hull, and underside cargo ramp or main personnel hatch.

The artwork uses hard-edged cel-shaded shadow boundaries with one or two flat shadow tones per surface, combined with subtle airbrushed gradients within the largest armor faces to give the metal weight and curvature. Bold black ink linework defines all silhouette edges, with finer ink for panel lines, rivet courses, and surface greebles. Line weight is heavier on the silhouette and finer on internal detail. The primary hull tone occupies roughly seventy percent of the surface area as the calm dominant color; a darker structural tone occupies roughly twenty-five percent, used inside engine bells, panel recesses, and shadowed zones beneath overhanging armor; a single saturated warning accent (red, orange, or yellow) occupies the remaining five percent, used sparingly for warning stripes, stenciled numbers, hazard chevrons, and one or two unit insignia. The hull palette is: Rusted orange, dull grey, caution yellow stripes.

The image is a clean reference sheet on a pure solid white background. Four orthographic views of the same vessel are arranged in a 2x2 grid at 1:1 aspect ratio: front view in the upper left, side view in the upper right, top-down view in the lower left, and three-quarter isometric view in the lower right. The four views are visually isolated from each other with no overlap and no panel separator lines drawn between them. The artwork is purely visual — no text, letters, numbers, labels, captions, arrows, callout lines, dimension markings, or any other typographic or diagrammatic overlay.
